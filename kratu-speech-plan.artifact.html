Before the plans

What was wrong on the live site, fixed today

The name bank never arrived

The console showed the name list returning 404. Without it the login could not match what a child said after "sunana" to a known name, which is the wonky microphone you saw. The file is now shipped with the site.

The identity engine never started

The face engine's script, its worker, the ONNX runtime and its models were not in the deployed folder, and nothing in the main flow initialised the engine anyway. All of it is deployed now, with the 174 MB face model swapped for the 14 MB one that GitHub allows, and the loader starts the engine before the first screen.

A loader that waits for everything

The Kratu word fills in letter by letter as five pieces arrive. The speech model now shows its download in megabytes, then "unpacking", because that piece is 41 MB and looked stuck on your first attempt. A failed piece shows why and can be retried; after two failed rounds an adult may continue anyway. Laila says "Don Allah a ɗan jira, ana shirya komai" at the start, immediately where the tablet allows sound before a tap and otherwise on the first touch.

Permission screen redesigned

One card, no greeting, no wordmark: what is needed, why each permission matters, tap Laila, tap Allow, and a line saying nothing is kept or sent.

Plan 1 · build

Laila listens to one child

Ten children at ten tablets in one room is the normal case. Today every tablet hears the whole room. The goal is that the tablet accepts only speech from the child who logged in, at the moment Laila asked, and ignores everything else.

What exists today

Mic gateThe microphone opens only while the child presses Laila, and Laila is never audible while it is open.
Browser cleanupThe stream asks for echo cancellation and noise suppression, mono, at the device rate (usually 48 kHz). A ScriptProcessor of 4096 samples feeds the recogniser and drives the amplitude ring on Laila.
Grammar recogniserVosk runs offline with a small grammar per step, so it only ever returns words from the current lesson. A neighbour saying "goat" during the goat question is still accepted.
Voice vectorEach child's login stores a voice embedding from the "sunana" clip, computed on device by the speaker model. Nothing uses it during lessons yet.

Plan 1b · in full

Speech activity detection, from the loader to the judged answer

Speech activity detection, VAD from here on, answers one question thirty times a second: is a person speaking into this microphone right now? Everything else in plan 1 hangs off that answer. Here is the whole design, piece by piece.

The model

Silero VADAn open model built for exactly this, about 2 MB as ONNX, trained on many languages and on children's voices among others. It takes 16 kHz audio in frames of 512 samples, which is 32 ms, and returns the probability that the frame contains speech. It keeps a small internal state between frames, which the caller passes back in.
RuntimeONNX Runtime Web is already shipped for the face engine, so no new library. The WebAssembly backend is enough: one frame costs about a millisecond on a cheap tablet, far below the 32 ms budget.
Where it livesmodels/vad/silero_vad.onnx, added to the deploy folder and to the deploy checklist, since every missing file today was a file that was never copied.

In the loader

The VAD becomes part of the "Murya · voice" piece, not a sixth letter. After the speech model has unpacked, the loader creates the VAD session and runs one silent frame through it to warm the runtime. If the VAD fails to load, the voice piece still succeeds and the app falls back to today's timers; a missing VAD is a degradation, not a stop. The loader records which of the two modes is active so the session log can say it.

The audio path, rebuilt

  1. Capture stays as it is: one mono stream with echo cancellation and noise suppression on.
  2. An AudioWorklet replaces the ScriptProcessor. A small worklet file, shipped alongside the app, runs on the audio thread, resamples the device rate down to 16 kHz, and posts fixed 512-sample frames to the main thread. ScriptProcessor is deprecated and stutters on cheap tablets; a worklet does not. Where a WebView is too old for worklets, the ScriptProcessor path stays as the fallback.
  3. Per frame, two numbers. The main thread computes the loudness of the frame and asks the VAD for its speech probability. Both are kept in a short ring buffer of the last 300 ms of frames, the pre-roll.
  4. The noise floor. During the first 300 ms after the mic opens, and rolling thereafter during non-speech, the 20th percentile of frame loudness is the room's floor. A frame counts as speech only if the VAD says so and it is at least 8 dB above the floor. The second condition is layer A; it is what stops a neighbour's quieter voice from triggering.
  5. Start. Three consecutive speech frames, about 100 ms, open the utterance. The pre-roll is fed to Vosk first, then live frames, so the first consonant of "goat" is not cut off.
  6. End. 700 ms of non-speech frames closes it. Vosk is asked for its final result, the mic closes, Laila's ring stops, and the answer is judged. A hard cap of 6 s per utterance guards against a child who keeps talking; 5 s with no start at all takes the existing "I did not hear" path.
  7. Speaker match hooks here. At end, the 16 kHz samples of the utterance go to the existing voice-embedding call, the result is compared with the logged-in child's vector by cosine similarity, and below the threshold the answer is refused with the "only you" line. The samples are discarded straight after, as at login.

Feeding Vosk

Today Vosk is fed the browser's AudioBuffer at the device rate. With frames already at 16 kHz the recogniser should take them directly; the shipped Vosk build must be checked for its float-input call, and if it lacks one, the AudioBuffer path is kept and the VAD is used only for gating and end-pointing. Either way the result is the same to the child: one clean utterance, no trailing silence, no timer.

What changes for the child

presses Laila→room measured, silently→speaks→ring stops the moment they stop→voice checked against their login→answer judged, or "kai kaɗai · only you"

The visible difference is speed. Today the mic waits out a timer after the child has finished; with VAD the judgement comes about a second after the last sound. That is also the number to measure: press-to-judgement time before and after.

Testing before the pilot

  • A local Chrome run with a fake microphone fed from recorded files: a child saying "goat" in silence, the same with room chatter mixed in, and chatter alone. The three files must give one accepted utterance, one accepted utterance, and nothing.
  • On the Galaxy Tab, ten presses in a quiet room and ten in a noisy one, timed.
  • The session log records mode, start and end times, floor, and the speaker score for every utterance, so a bad day can be diagnosed from records rather than by watching.

Plan 2 · build

"Shikin" is chicken

A Hausa-speaking child learning English will say certain sounds the Hausa way. Some of those are not mistakes worth marking; they are the accent every Nigerian English speaker has. Laila should accept them, and where it matters, model the standard sound once without a red mark.

What exists today

Exact matchThe recogniser is given the expected words as its grammar, and the answer is matched by substring and a plain edit distance. "Shikin" is two letters from "chicken", which is treated like any two-letter error.
Three triesQuizzes allow three attempts and never reveal the answer before the results screen. Accent tolerance fits inside that.
No record of what was heardAttempts are logged as right or wrong. The recognised text is not kept, so the table below cannot yet be built from data. Logging it is the first task.

Plan 2b · in full

Sound skeleton scoring

Letters are the wrong unit for judging speech. "Chicken" and "shikin" differ in three letters and one sound. The skeleton turns both the expected word and what the recogniser returned into a short string of sound symbols, then measures the distance between the two with costs that know which differences are accent and which are a different word.

Step 1 · Clean the recognised text

Lower-case, strip punctuation, collapse spaces. Where the recogniser returned several words, every window of one to three words is tried and the best score wins, so "the goat" still matches "goat".

Step 2 · Rewrite to sounds

An ordered list of rewrites, applied to both strings. The order matters: digraphs before single letters, silent letters before vowel classes.

ch→C   sh→S   th→T   ph→f   ck→k   qu→kw   wh→w   x→ks
ee, ea, ie → i      oo → u      ou, ow → o      ai, ay, ey → e
final silent e dropped (goose→gus)     doubled letters collapsed (pepper→peper)
c→k before a,o,u; c→s before e,i,y      y as a vowel → i
every remaining vowel → one of a e i o u
chicken → Cikin      shikin → Sikin      school → skul      sukul → sukul
seven → seven        seben → seben       three → Tri        tri → tri

Step 3 · Distance with accent-aware costs

A standard edit distance, except that the cost of a substitution depends on the pair. The table is the accent table above, written as numbers, and it lives in one JSON file that the app and the test script both read.

DifferenceCostWhy
Same symbol0
Listed accent pair: C↔S, p↔f, v↔b, T↔t, T↔d, z↔s0.15The Hausa ear, not an error
Vowel for vowel0.30Vowel quality varies with every accent
A vowel inserted between two consonants0.20Cluster breaking, sukul for skul
Final consonant dropped0.50Common, but "goa" for goat is only just acceptable
Any other substitution, insertion or deletion1.00A different word

Step 4 · Normalise and decide

The distance is divided by the length of the expected skeleton, with a floor of three so that short words are not judged too harshly. Then three bands.

Step 5 · The contrast lock

A lesson that is teaching the difference between two words must not forgive the difference it is teaching. Each lesson item may name its contrast pairs. When "ship" is asked and "sheep" is a named contrast, the vowel cost for that item becomes 1.00, and "sheep" is far. Nothing else changes.

Step 6 · Help the recogniser hear the variants

Vosk with a grammar can only return words it was told to listen for, so the skeleton alone is not enough: if only "chicken" is in the grammar, "shikin" is either forced to "chicken", which is fine, or to nothing. The table is run backwards to generate spellings of each expected word as a Hausa speaker would say it, at most eight per word, and those are added to the grammar. Variants that are not in the model's own vocabulary are dropped, because the recogniser cannot produce them anyway. The child's word then comes back as the variant it actually was, the skeleton scores it near, and the log records the true form.

Step 7 · Log, then rebuild the table from the pilot

Every attempt stores the expected word, the recognised text, the skeleton score and the outcome, in the child's record alongside right or wrong. After a month the recognised forms of every word are counted, and the substitutions the children actually make replace the guesses above. The thresholds move with them. A facilitator listens to twenty near cases to confirm nothing wrong has crept in.

Where the code goes

  • One JSON file with the rewrites, costs and thresholds, read by the app and by a Python test script that runs the worked examples above and fails if any band changes.
  • One scoring function used by every module in place of the current substring-plus-edit-distance match.
  • The grammar builder in the speech engine gains the variant generator.
  • The attempt log gains the recognised text and score.

Rules that hold everywhere

  • A child never passes a lesson and fails a test for the same accent. One table, one threshold, both places.
  • The results screen does not distinguish near from exact. A child who said "shikin" was right.
  • The modelling line plays at most once per word per session. The second near on the same word is a silent tick.
  • Near never applies while a contrast lock is active for that item.
  • The carry list is untouched: near is not a miss, so nothing returns.

Plan 2d · in full

New Laila lines: recording, review, release

Every plan above adds lines, and every line follows the same path that the existing 640 clips took, so nothing reaches a child before you have heard it.

  1. Draft. The lines below, boy and girl forms, with an English caption for the subtitles. A north-east Hausa speaker corrects the wording before anything is recorded. "Saurara" and "saurari" are the imperative to a boy and to a girl; the two forms are not optional.
  2. Takes. Two or three takes per line from the voice pipeline, each normalised to the same loudness as the rest of the app and encoded the same way, so a new line never sounds louder or thinner than its neighbours.
  3. Review round. A new round on the voices page: one row per line, the takes side by side with play buttons, the text under each, accept or reject per take, and a note field. The round also carries the clip that plays after the modelling line, so you hear the join: "Daidai ne. Saurara:" then "chicken", with the 350 ms gap the app will use.
  4. Release. The accepted take is written into the app's audio map, the manifest, the Hausa text file and the English caption file in one step, and a transcript audit confirms the recorded audio says what the text says. Lines with no accepted take stay out of the build; the code path plays nothing rather than a rejected take.
  5. Where they play. Near modelling: the line, a short gap, then the existing word clip; never in tests. Only-you: after a speaker mismatch, once, then the mic reopens. Break and whole: the syllable flow, when built.

Plan 3 · plan only, nothing built

Breaking a word into pieces

In the reading lesson, when a child cannot say a word after two tries, Laila breaks it into syllables, says each one, has the child say each one back, then the whole word. It is how a teacher does it at a desk.

misses "camel" twice→Laila: "mu raba shi · let's break it"→ca·mel, first piece lit→Laila says "ca", child says "ca"→ca·mel, again→"yanzu duka · now all of it"→child says "camel", Madalla

The whole-word attempt at the end counts as the third try. If it fails, the word goes to the carry list as today. It only runs inside the reading lesson, never in a test.

Syllable splitsHand-written into the word file for the fixed English set; Hausa words split by rule and checked by hand in an hour.
Audio per pieceEach syllable in Laila's voice and the two child voices, same pipeline, same loudness. Roughly 300 short clips.
ScreenThe Rubutu slate already draws a word as slots; the active piece in colour, the rest grey, the pointing hand under it.
RecognitionSingle syllables are checked with the loudness gate and the skeleton, not exact recognition; the whole word at the end as now.

Open decisions for you

  • Two misses before breaking, or one?
  • Reading lesson only, or also Abubuwa?
  • Does a syllable step count for progress, or only the whole word? This note assumes only the whole word.
