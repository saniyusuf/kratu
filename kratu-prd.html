Executive Summary

What Kratu is, who it serves, and why it deserves funding — in one page.

Kratu is a self-contained learning application that teaches foundational English literacy — letters, sounds, vocabulary, spelling, and reading — to children who speak Hausa and cannot yet read in any language. It runs entirely offline on an ordinary tablet, is operated by voice and touch rather than text, and grounds every lesson in photographs and words drawn from the child's own environment in northern Nigeria.

The learning experience is anchored by Laila, a friendly on-screen guide. A child hears an instruction in Hausa, taps Laila, speaks a word in English, and Kratu — using on-device speech recognition — tells them instantly whether they were understood. No teacher needs to be literate in English; no lesson needs a network connection; no child needs to be able to read a single word to begin.

A working prototype exists today: a single-file web application with staged A–Z alphabet lessons, thirteen vocabulary domains, spelling and reading modes, gender-aware voice guidance, and fully on-device speech recognition. This grant funds the path from prototype to a field-ready learning programme — content depth, a hardened offline installer, a facilitator toolkit, a supervised pilot at a physical learning centre, and an independent evaluation of learning gains.

The Problem & Context

A literacy emergency, concentrated where infrastructure is thinnest and mother-tongue tools are rarest.

Nigeria carries one of the largest out-of-school and low-literacy populations in the world, and that burden is not spread evenly. It falls hardest on the north, on rural communities, and on girls — precisely the children for whom conventional, text-first, connected, English-medium edtech is least usable.

~10–18M‡
Out-of-school children in Nigeria — among the highest counts globally
50M+‡
First-language Hausa speakers across West Africa
North‡
Region where out-of-school and low-literacy rates concentrate
Girls‡
Disproportionately excluded from foundational literacy

Why existing tools do not reach these children

  • They assume literacy to teach literacy. Most learning apps require the child to read menus, buttons, and instructions — a barrier for the very children who most need to learn to read.
  • They assume connectivity. Streamed video, cloud accounts, and online speech services fail in communities with intermittent or absent internet and unreliable power.
  • They assume English as the language of instruction, leaving a Hausa-speaking child with no bridge from what they know to what they are learning.
  • They assume an adult teacher fluent in English is present and available for one-to-one practice — a scarce resource in under-resourced classrooms.
  • They are culturally distant — illustrations, names, and examples drawn from contexts the child has never seen.
The gap Kratu fills A learning tool that needs no reading ability, no internet, no English-literate adult, and no cultural translation — because it is voice-first, offline-first, mother-tongue-scaffolded, and built from the child's own world.

Product Overview

Kratu presents as a warm, calm, full-screen application. A child chooses their profile, and is then guided entirely by voice through a set of learning modes that build on one another. Every screen has a consistent grammar: a friendly guide who speaks, a large picture, and one or two large touch targets — never a wall of text or a cluttered menu.

Guided by Laila

A 3-D animated mascot who gives instructions, invites the child to speak, and celebrates success — the single, consistent point of interaction.

Spoken, not written

All instruction is audio in Hausa; the target language (English) is heard and spoken. Reading is the goal, never the entry requirement.

Answered by voice

The child speaks into the device and is understood on-device — turning passive tapping into active production.

Design north star A four-year-old who has never seen a screen should be able to succeed at their first lesson without a single word of written instruction.

Learning Model & Pedagogy

A tight, repeatable practice loop, staged so difficulty rises only as mastery grows.

The core practice loop

1 · Hear
Listen

Kratu names the item in Hausa, then models the English word.

2 · See
Anchor

A photograph from the child's world fixes meaning to the sound.

3 · Choose
Decide

"Do you know it in English?" — tap Laila to try, or the board to be taught.

4 · Speak
Produce

The child says the word aloud; the device listens on-device.

5 · Know
Verify

Instant, encouraging feedback — correct, or gently modelled again.

Pedagogical foundations

  • Mother-tongue scaffolding. New English is always bridged from Hausa the child already owns — reducing cognitive load and honouring their first language.
  • Active production over passive recognition. The child speaks; spoken production, verified in the moment, builds durable phonological memory that tap-only quizzes cannot.
  • Immediate, judgement-free feedback. Every attempt is met at once — celebrated or re-modelled — never punished. Repetition is safe.
  • Staged mastery. Content is chunked and gated: the alphabet is taught in small groups; vocabulary is learned then mixed then tested; each stage unlocks the next.
  • Dual coding. Sound + image + spoken production together encode each item through multiple channels.
  • Low-stimulation calm. No timers, scores, or loud game mechanics that overwhelm a first-time learner — attention stays on the word.

Progression

Stage 1
Letters

Recognise and voice A–Z in small taught groups, then mixed and tested.

Stage 2
Words

Learn vocabulary by domain: hear → identify → speak → verify.

Stage 3
Spelling

Hear a word, then build it letter-by-letter by voice.

Stage 4
Reading

Decode whole words aloud — the first act of reading.

Experience & Accessibility

Principles that make the product usable by a non-literate first-time user.

No reading to learn to read

Every action is reachable by listening and tapping. Written labels are decorative reinforcement, never the only path.

One decision per screen

Large targets, generous spacing, and at most a couple of choices keep a first-time learner from ever being lost.

Consistent grammar

The same guide, in the same place, doing the same thing on every screen — predictability lowers anxiety and builds independence.

Forgiving & patient

No penalties, timers, or dead-ends. A child can replay, retry, or step back at any moment, endlessly.

Low-power, low-stimulation

Calm colour, minimal motion, no jarring sound — kinder on attention and on battery.

Robust on modest hardware

Designed for entry-level tablets and small screens, with touch targets sized for small hands.

Kratu is a framework-free, single-artifact web application. There is no server, no account system, and no cloud dependency in the learning path: the interface, lesson logic, speech recogniser, audio, and images all live inside the package and execute on the device. Being built on the open web platform means it runs across the widest range of low-cost hardware without per-platform rebuilds.

11.2 · On-device speech recognition

The enabling technology — and the hardest engineering — is recognising a child's speech entirely on the device, with no connectivity. Kratu uses a Vosk speech recogniser (built on the Kaldi engine) compiled to WebAssembly, with a compact acoustic model bundled in the app. Audio is captured through the browser's Web Audio pipeline and streamed to the recogniser frame-by-frame; nothing is ever transmitted off the device.

ComponentDetail
EngineVosk (Kaldi-based) offline recogniser, compiled to WebAssembly for the browser.
ModelCompact bundled acoustic model (≈ 40 MB), loaded once from local storage.
Audio captureWeb Audio API — single-channel, echo cancellation and noise suppression on; frames streamed live to the recogniser.
Grammar constraintThe recogniser is restricted to the expected tokens for the current task (the letters in a group, or a domain's words). This sharply raises accuracy on child speech and cuts compute.
Matching layerRecognised text is mapped to the target with tolerant matching; up to a few forgiving retries before the word is re-modelled.
PrivacyAll processing is local. No audio is recorded to disk, transmitted, or sent to any service.
Design consequence Because recognition is grammar-constrained per lesson, Kratu can be both accurate and fast on modest hardware — it only ever has to decide between a small, known set of expected answers, not transcribe open speech.

11.3 · Guidance audio (speech out)

Every instruction, letter, and word the child hears is a pre-generated voice clip, bundled inside the app as encoded audio so playback never touches the network. Two languages run in parallel: Hausa for all guidance, and English as the target being taught. Clips are selected at runtime, including gender-aware variants so instructions are grammatically correct for the child (Hausa distinguishes, e.g., ka / ki).

Current build

Guidance audio is produced from text-to-speech, layered and packaged by a build step, and embedded directly in the app as encoded clips.

Production path

For final quality and clean licensing, the programme moves guidance audio to native-speaker recordings (a grant content deliverable, §9/§19), keeping the same runtime.

11.4 · Visual content pipeline

Kratu's pictures are photorealistic and culturally specific — a goat, not a moose; local food, homes, and people. They are produced with modern text-to-image diffusion models (FLUX family) run locally, from a curated prompt library, then reviewed for cultural fit and pronunciation alignment.

StageDetail
GenerationFLUX diffusion models (a higher-fidelity model for finals, a fast model for iteration) run locally on Apple-silicon hardware, driven by a versioned prompt library.
CurationEvery image is reviewed by native speakers/educators for accuracy and appropriateness before it enters the library.
OptimisationDownscaled to ≈ 640 px on the long edge and re-encoded to WebP (~q90), alpha-safe and visually lossless — small files for constrained storage.
PackagingOptimised images are embedded in the app so they load instantly and fully offline.

11.5 · Lesson engine & data model

Content is data, not code. Vocabulary domains, the alphabet, and each mode's sequence are described declaratively; the same engine drives every mode. This is what makes new languages and regions a content exercise rather than a rebuild.

  • Content model. Each domain is a list of items; an item carries its written word, its image, and the keys of its Hausa and English audio clips. The alphabet is grouped into small taught sets.
  • Phase machine. A mode advances through phases — for words: learn → mixed → test; the alphabet: taught group → mixed → full test; plus dedicated spelling, reading, and listen-and-find phases.
  • Mastery gating. Progress unlocks the next chunk; verified spoken answers, not mere taps, move the child forward.
  • Progress store. Per-child profiles and mastery are persisted locally on the device (browser storage), keyed by profile — so many children share one tablet with independent progress and no personal data collected.

11.6 · Offline delivery, storage & footprint

Kratu ships as a self-contained artifact with all media inlined, so it runs from local storage with no network. Installable offline caching (service worker / PWA) so it launches like a native app from the home screen is a near-term hardening deliverable (§14, Phase 2).

11.7 · Interface, rendering & the mascot

The interface is standard web UI, tuned for a first-time, non-literate user: large targets, one decision per screen, calm colour, and reduced-motion awareness. Laila — the guide the child taps to speak — is a single vector (SVG) mascot with gradient 3-D shading and lightweight CSS animation (an idle breathe and a blink). Being vector, she is razor-sharp at any screen size and costs almost nothing in storage or battery. Pressing Laila directly switches on the listener: she is the microphone, so there is never a separate control to hunt for.

11.9 · Engineering & content tooling

Behind the app sits a small toolchain that keeps content reproducible — so expanding the curriculum is a repeatable process, not bespoke work each time:

Audio toolchain

Scripts synthesise/prepare voice clips per language, merge audio layers, and maintain a manifest of every clip — making it straightforward to add or re-voice content.

Image toolchain

A prompt library plus a resumable render driver generate images locally; a compression step standardises every picture to the WebP target.

Content as data

Domains, items, and lessons are defined in structured data files, so new vocabulary drops in without touching the engine.

Reproducible builds

Media and manifests are version-controlled, so any release can be rebuilt and audited — important for a fundable, maintainable programme.

11.10 · Engineering principles

  • Offline-first, not offline-tolerant. The network is used only for optional install/updates — never to deliver a lesson.
  • Single-artifact simplicity. A self-contained package a facilitator can side-load and run, minimising IT overhead at the centre.
  • Cross-platform by default. Open web technology runs on the widest range of low-cost devices without per-platform rebuilds.
  • Swappable content & language layers. Audio, images, and vocabulary are data — the path to new languages without re-engineering.
  • Privacy by construction. The architecture collects essentially nothing; the speech pipeline never leaves the device.
Build status The core enabling technology — real speech recognition running entirely offline on low-cost hardware — is implemented and working in the current prototype. The engineering scope funded by this grant is hardening (installable offline packaging, provisioning, facilitator reporting) and content, not unproven core technology.

Privacy, Safety & Data

Child-safe by construction — the safest data is the data never collected.

Voice never leaves the device

Speech recognition runs entirely on-device. No child's voice recording is transmitted, stored remotely, or sent to any third party.

No accounts, no personal data

Profiles use a first name or avatar only. No email, phone, location, or identifying data is required or collected.

No ads, no tracking, no in-app purchases

There is no advertising, behavioural tracking, or commercial content of any kind directed at children.

Local, exportable progress

Progress data stays on the device and can be summarised for the facilitator; aggregate, de-identified metrics support evaluation.

Alignment The design is built to respect children's-data and privacy principles (e.g. data minimisation and purpose limitation) by collecting essentially nothing — the final programme will complete a safeguarding and data-protection review with the host organisation before pilot.

Monitoring & Evaluation

How we will know Kratu works — and prove it to funders.

#IndicatorMethodTarget
M1Letter recognition gainPre/post one-to-one assessment[e.g. +X letters]
M2Spoken-word vocabulary gainPre/post picture-naming task[e.g. +X words]

Approach

  • Baseline & endline. Each child is assessed on entry and again after the pilot, with the difference as the primary learning measure.
  • Built-in dosage data. The app's own usage summaries provide objective practice-time evidence without extra instrumentation or any personal data.
  • Mixed methods. Quantitative gains are paired with facilitator and (where appropriate) caregiver interviews for the "why" behind the numbers.
  • Independent analysis. Evaluation is designed with, and reviewed by, an external partner to ensure credibility.

Sustainability & Scaling

Low marginal cost

Once built and installed, additional lessons and additional children cost effectively nothing — the economics improve with scale.

Replicable template

Content and language are data layers. A new language or region reuses the entire engine — the biggest cost is content, not code.

Partner-delivered

Delivery rides on existing community, NGO, and faith-based networks rather than requiring new institutions.

Open, durable technology

Built on open web standards with no proprietary lock-in, keeping long-term maintenance affordable.

Beyond the grant

  • Prove it once, template it many times. A successful, evaluated pilot becomes the reference model for replication across regions and languages.
  • Diversify funding. Pilot evidence unlocks follow-on philanthropic, institutional, and government channels for scale-up.
  • Grow the content library collaboratively with local educators, compounding value for every future deployment.
